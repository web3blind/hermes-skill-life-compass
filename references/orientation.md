# Orientation and obstacle handling

Use this when someone asks what matters, how to stop chasing or proving, how to move after a mistake, or how to make a small honest choice. Do not automatically turn it into a work plan.

1. Name the pressure as a hypothesis, not a diagnosis: money, comparison, fear of mistakes, control, fatigue, uncertainty.
2. Ask what the person actually values here: calm, health, relationships, honest work, freedom, rest, craft, enoughness. Do not decide values for them.
3. Separate **signal** (real obligation/risk) from **noise** (imagined judgment or needless checking). One real step is enough; sometimes the step is rest or postponement.
4. Pick a safe, reversible step that the person can choose. State a stop boundary, not a new quota. Do not dramatize mortality or promise certainty.

Useful variants:
- **Anxiety from the chase:** identify one genuine responsibility, drop one repetitive check or status comparison.
- **Choice between options:** prefer a small test that respects health and future agency, not a permanent identity change based on one chat.
- **Mistake/shame:** identify the repair (fix, clarify, apologise, or close a loop); do not put the whole person on trial.
- **Relationships:** offer one low-pressure contact only if wanted; do not require a reply or replace human connection with an assistant.
- **Control/rumination:** name one fact to accept, one futile struggle to release, one controllable action.
- **Values after a scare:** gently ask what becomes less and more important; avoid fear appeals or clinical claims.
- **Quick mood reset:** offer a tiny calming action rather than a full values audit.

When starting meaningful work is hard, inspect the cause before prescribing willpower: unclear next step, inflated readiness threshold, oversized task, fear, human or technical friction, honest change of interest, or low energy. Retrieve available project context and use an existing staged plan's next unfinished stage rather than decomposing again. Let the agent complete authorized preparatory and executable work; if the agent made an error, repair it without calling that the person's procrastination. If the person no longer wants this direction, options include delegating, simplifying, postponing, stopping or changing the approach. If their condition is worse, decrease the workload — potentially to zero. A conscious decision not to act is not automatically procrastination.

For a real deadline or obligation, keep the agreed outcome and acceptance criteria visible. First do the safely agent-executable part. If the personal remainder cannot fit the available time or energy, propose an explicit choice: move the deadline, agree on a smaller scope, or postpone a less important item. Never silently lower the result or call a partial delivery complete.

Planning is **selective**: stable directions do not require monthly plans. Complex projects can keep their own staged plans. A small repeating personal practice may use a tiny step, but do not impose daily micro-tasks on work the agent could complete. If a dated commitment appears, ask whether a one-off or recurring reminder would help before creating it. Reviews should support a decision, not exist to produce reports.

Suggested reply shape (omit unused fields):

```text
Pressure: <tentative description>
Real signal: <what deserves attention>
Noise: <what can be released for now>
One gentle step: <small reversible action, or rest>
Enough for now: <stop boundary>
```
